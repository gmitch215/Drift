I’m a first-year student at Dartmouth who has been coding since I was seven and working with Kotlin since I was eleven. Although I hadn’t built a full-stack Kotlin project in over a year, this contest gave me a chance to reconnect with my favorite programming language.

My developer history is characterized by “when something is missing, fix it.” In eighth grade, I built MobChip to standardize Minecraft pathfinding through obfuscated internals. My first Kotlin projects, PlasmaEnchants and BattleCards, were Minecraft projects for friends. Later, my debate team used my multiplatform TabroomAPI to map opponents. With Combinatory, a KorGE game, I spent so much time fixing engine issues that I was almost an upstream maintainer. After over 500 hours of Kotlin, I moved toward harder systems projects, but Kotlin remained in my toolchains.

One problem kept resurfacing: why did code work on my Mac but fail in GitHub Actions? Why did my website work in Firefox but not Safari or Chrome?

I built Drift with Kotlin Multiplatform to investigate those differences. I designed its roadmap around comparing environments, language behavior, and tooling, starting with my Mac and CI runners. Claude handled much of the raw implementation; I verified its research and measured the differences. I expanded Drift to Android, iOS, and desktop targets, using my remote Linux machine, paisley-park, to calibrate its model. Drift surfaced Kotlin standard-library differences across JVM, JS, and Native, which I reported through YouTrack. I used Claude to assemble a Compose Multiplatform Atlas showing behavior across platforms and versions. I extended Drift’s diagnostics so you can ask why something fails: it compares measurements and error reports against rules, explains the evidence, and suggests what to check next, all without an LLM.

Drift aims to solve one of software’s oldest puzzles: “it works on my machine.”
