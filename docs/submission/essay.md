I’m a first-year student at Dartmouth who has been coding since I was seven and working with Kotlin since I was eleven. This contest gave me a chance to reconnect with my favorite programming language.

My developer history is characterized by "when something is missing, fix it." In eighth grade, I built MobChip to standardize Minecraft pathfinding through obfuscated internals. PlasmaEnchants and BattleCards were Kotlin projects for friends. Later, my debate team used my multiplatform TabroomAPI to map opponents. With Combinatory, a KorGE game, I spent so much time fixing engine issues that I was almost an upstream maintainer. I moved toward harder systems projects, but Kotlin remained in my toolchains.

One problem kept resurfacing: why did code work on my Mac but fail in GitHub Actions? Why did my website work in Firefox but not Safari or Chrome?

I built Drift with Kotlin Multiplatform to investigate those differences. Shared Kotlin diagnostics let me run the same tests across platforms; the Atlas compares their actual behavior. I designed its roadmap around comparing environments, language behavior, and tooling, starting with my Mac and CI runners. Claude handled much of the raw implementation; I designed the experiments, verified findings, and measured differences. I expanded Drift to Android, iOS, and desktop targets, using my remote Linux machine, paisley-park, to calibrate its model. Drift surfaced Kotlin standard-library differences across JVM, JS, and Native, which I reported through YouTrack. Claude helped assemble the Compose Multiplatform Atlas across versions. I extended Drift’s diagnostics so you can ask why something fails: it compares measurements and error reports against rules, explains the evidence, and suggests what to check next, all without an LLM.

Drift aims to solve one of software’s oldest puzzles: "it works on my machine."
